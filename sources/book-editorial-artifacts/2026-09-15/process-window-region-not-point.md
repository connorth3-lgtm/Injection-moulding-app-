# Process window: a region supported by multiple outcomes

Status: Book-ready worked-example specification. Research/editorial only; not learner-runtime content.

## Teaching purpose

A process window is not one “best” machine setting. The Book should teach learners to identify a robust operating region that satisfies several outcomes at once and to understand how that region changes when quality priorities change.

## Figure composition

Create an original two-factor evidence grid. The final Book version may use injection speed versus packing pressure, melt temperature versus mould temperature, or another pair justified by the selected case study.

Divide the grid into cells or regions and classify each as:

- **acceptable across all selected responses**;
- **acceptable for one response but rejected by another**;
- **unstable / high-variation region**;
- **not tested**.

Add at least three response layers such as:

1. dimensional result;
2. defect / fill result;
3. process-variation or pressure result.

The visual should make it impossible to mistake one optimum point for the full operating window.

## Core teaching rule

> **The useful target is a defensible region where the important responses remain acceptable—not the setting that maximises one response while sacrificing another.**

## Worked example structure

Give learners a simplified synthetic grid derived from the method, not copied source data. Label it **synthetic teaching grid — method illustrated from experimental process-window studies**.

Example qualitative cells:

| Region | Fill/short-shot response | Dimensional response | Pressure/process stability | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | reject | acceptable | stable | outside window |
| B | acceptable | acceptable | stable | robust candidate |
| C | acceptable | reject | stable | outside window |
| D | acceptable | acceptable | unstable | conditional / investigate |
| E | flash/overpack risk | reject | high pressure | outside window |

## Learner exercise

### Scenario 1 — dimensional priority

A customer tightens a dimensional requirement while the short-shot requirement stays unchanged. Ask learners which existing cells remain viable and what additional confirmation runs are needed around the new boundary.

### Scenario 2 — robustness priority

A region gives the best average dimension but large shot-to-shot variation. Another region gives a slightly less ideal average dimension but much lower variation and remains inside specification.

Ask learners which region they would choose for production and why.

### Expected reasoning

A strong answer considers multiple responses, interactions and variation. It should prefer a robust region rather than blindly selecting the nominal setting with the best mean value.

Learners should also state that a window established on one machine/material/mould combination is not automatically transferable to another.

## Editorial boundaries

- Experimental process windows illustrate method, not universal recipes.
- Do not turn a study's tested factor levels into recommended production settings for unrelated materials or tools.
- Show interactions and rejected regions where evidence permits; avoid presenting only the final optimum.
- Keep supplier/grade limits and safety constraints controlling where applicable.
- Synthetic teaching grids must be clearly labelled as synthetic.
